Can a language model read the numbers in an ICU chart?

MD6329 Deep Learning for Healthcare AI · Mini-project · October 2026

Open LecTrace presentation (opens in a new tab) Download report (opens in a new tab)

Question. Should we fine-tune a large language model built for health records, or train a small model from scratch? We compared Llemr, a 7-billion-parameter EHR language model, with a 0.29-million-parameter event encoder on one standard benchmark: predicting in-hospital death from the first 48 hours in the ICU. The protocol was fixed before any Llemr result existed, and three diagnostics then asked how each model uses the measured values.

About 9 minutes to read.

TL;DR

  1. The small model wins. Test AUROC is 0.882 against 0.795 on 1,199 held-out patients (difference −0.086, 95% CI −0.119 to −0.057). AUPRC is 0.539 against 0.409. A longer Llemr run, requested after the result was known (post hoc), reached 0.782 and did not close the gap.
  2. It is far cheaper. It trains in 30 s on one GPU, where Llemr needs 4,539 s: about 150 times the time, 1,200 times the trained parameters and 25,000 times the total parameters.
  3. The gap points to how numbers enter the model. Shuffle the measured values across patients and both models fall to about 0.66 to 0.67 AUROC. Raise one lactate value from 1 to 8 mmol/L and the small model’s mean risk rises 1.08 points, while Llemr’s does not rise. The frozen text encoder gives almost the same vector for “Lactate 1.5” and “Lactate 8” (cosine 0.996).
  4. The scope is narrow. One dataset, one split, one seed and one fine-tuning recipe. The diagnostics are consistent with a numeracy problem but do not prove one, and nothing here says that language models are worse in general.

Fine-tune a large pretrained model, or train a small one from scratch?

An ICU records dozens of measurements per patient at irregular times: heart rate, creatinine, lactate and more. Predicting who will die in hospital from the first 48 hours supports triage and resource planning. It is a standard benchmark for models of clinical time series (PhysioNet 2012, 11,988 patients, 36 variables).

Language models are now offered as general solutions for health records. Llemr, from NeurIPS 2024, writes every clinical event as text and reads it with a 7-billion-parameter model. It is reported to reach results comparable to state-of-the-art baselines after fine-tuning for mortality prediction. We asked how it fares against a small, purpose-built model trained from scratch on the same patients.

A first pilot put a general-purpose language model (Qwen3-0.6B) on top of our encoder. It did not beat the encoder alone: mean test AUROC over three splits was 0.871 frozen and 0.864 LoRA-tuned, against 0.875. That left open whether a model pretrained on clinical events would do better.

How we kept the comparison honest

  1. Fix the protocol. The specification was hashed with SHA-256 before any Llemr result existed. Later changes are logged as amendments with their timing. c637e496b060adc7b93689cb779518ff8b0142ae6ca3a98c7adafdd643c53ab0
  2. Train both models. Same patients, same events, same static information, same test set.
  3. Test once. Each model is evaluated on the test set a single time. A paired bootstrap with 1,000 resamples compares them.
  4. Diagnose. Three probes ask how each model uses the numbers: shuffle the values, raise one lactate value, and test the text encoder.

Decision rule. If the 95% interval of the AUROC difference excludes 0, one model is better. Otherwise there is no detectable difference.

AUROC is the chance that a patient who died gets a higher risk than one who survived, so 0.5 is chance. AUPRC suits the 14% death rate.

A longer fine-tuning run for Llemr was started after this result was known (post hoc). It reached 0.7824 AUROC and 0.3782 AUPRC and did not close the gap. It is not the headline.

Shuffle the values, keep everything else

For every variable we permute its measured values across the test patients. Each patient keeps the same variables, counts and times, so only the numbers are wrong. Both models get the same shuffle. If a model uses the numbers, this should cost it much of its accuracy. What remains comes from which tests were taken and when, and from the static facts.

Both models fall to about 0.66 to 0.67. The real values are worth +0.221 AUROC to the small model and +0.122 to Llemr. AUPRC falls from 0.539 to 0.203 for the small model and from 0.409 to 0.258 for Llemr.

Raise one lactate value, watch the risk

For the 633 test patients with a lactate measurement, we set the latest lactate to 1, 2, 4, 6 and 8 mmol/L and averaged the predicted risk. Higher lactate is a known marker of higher mortality (normal is below about 2 mmol/L), so a model that reads the number should not lower its risk.

At 8 mmol/L the small model’s mean risk is 1.08 points higher than at 1. Llemr’s is 0.12 points lower.

Why might Llemr miss the numbers?

Llemr writes each event as a sentence and passes it to a frozen BiomedBERT text encoder. We compared the encoder’s vectors for low, normal and high values of lactate, mean blood pressure and heart rate. A cosine of 1 means the same vector.

0.9958“Lactate 1.5” against “Lactate 8 mmol/L”. Almost the same vector.
0.9969Average cosine for the same variable with a different value (9 pairs).
0.9435Average cosine for different variables (27 pairs).

Changing the value moves the vector less than changing the variable does. The value also reaches the model as one raw, unnormalised number beside the vector. These probes are consistent with a numeracy problem. They do not prove one.

Llemr was not overfitting. Its training loss fell only from 1.18 to about 1.0, where a constant predictor scores 1.19. The small model’s fell from 1.06 to 0.68.

What this shows, and what it does not

The evidence supports

  • On PhysioNet 2012 split 1, with Llemr-v1 fine-tuned as in its paper, the small model scored higher on AUROC and AUPRC. The interval of the difference excludes 0.
  • The numbers carry the signal. With the values shuffled, both models fall to about 0.66 to 0.67 AUROC.
  • Llemr’s predicted risk does not rise with lactate, and the small model’s does. This is consistent with a numeracy problem in how numbers reach Llemr.
  • Training Llemr for longer did not help. The post hoc 5-epoch run reached 0.782 AUROC.
  • For health care, check a model with counterfactuals (change one input and watch the output), not only with AUROC.

It does not support

  • That fine-tuning language models is worse in general. This is one dataset, one split and one seed for Llemr, one language model and one recipe without tuning.
  • That the diagnostics prove why Llemr scored lower. A second handicap is a data mismatch. 84.7% of the events are bedside values such as vital signs, which Llemr’s released training events did not include, and its lab sentences always ended in “normal” or “abnormal”, which this data does not have.
  • State of the art. The small model is in the top tier of published results, and differences below about one point are within the variation between papers.
  • Calibrated risks. Both models were trained with a class weight, so their probabilities are uncalibrated. The study is retrospective.
